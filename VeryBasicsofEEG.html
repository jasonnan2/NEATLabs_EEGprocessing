Finding the brain signal in a noisy recording

A scalp EEG electrode records a few microvolts of brain activity buried under blinks, muscle tension, mains hum and slow drift. This tutorial covers three tools for dealing with that, each with something you can drag around.

1. Averaging trials

When you present the same stimulus many times, the brain’s response is roughly the same each time, time-locked to the stimulus. The noise is not: it is different on every trial. Cut the recording into epochs around each stimulus and average them. The response adds up coherently, while noise that is random and zero-mean partly cancels.

If single-trial noise has standard deviation σ and the trials are independent, the noise left after averaging N trials is about σ/√N. Four times the trials only halves the noise, so gains slow down quickly.

The true response here is a small negative dip near 110 ms followed by a larger positive peak near 350 ms, similar in shape to the N1 and P3 components of an event-related potential (ERP). Try raising the noise: the more trials you need, the noisier the single trial is.

Averaging only works if the assumptions hold. If the response latency jitters from trial to trial, the average is smeared and smaller. If an artifact (a blink, say) happens on many trials at the same moment, it will not average away, so bad epochs are usually rejected or corrected before averaging.

2. Frequency filtering

Any trace can be described as a sum of oscillations at different frequencies. Different sources of signal and noise live in different frequency ranges, so a filter that keeps some frequencies and removes others can clean the data without needing repeated trials.

  • High-pass removes frequencies below the cutoff. It kills drift but, set too high, distorts slow ERP components.
  • Low-pass removes frequencies above the cutoff. It tames muscle and hiss but, set too low, blunts fast peaks.
  • Notch removes a narrow band, typically mains hum. Use it sparingly, since it also removes real activity at that frequency.
  • Band-pass is a high-pass and low-pass together, as in the alpha preset.

Two practical notes. First, a filter applied once forwards in time shifts the waveform; running it forwards and backwards (zero-phase, as done here) avoids that delay, though it can smear activity slightly earlier in time. Second, filter the continuous recording before cutting it into epochs, because short epochs have edge effects that distort the slow end of the spectrum.

Filtering and averaging complement each other: filters remove noise at frequencies outside your signal, while averaging also reduces noise that overlaps the signal’s frequencies.

3. Electrode positions

Electrodes sit on a standard layout called the 10–20 system, named for placing them at 10% or 20% of the distance between landmarks such as the nasion and inion. Names tell you where: the letter gives the region (Fp frontal pole, F frontal, C central, T temporal, P parietal, O occipital), odd numbers are on the left, even on the right and z is the midline.

Position matters because each electrode mostly sees the cortex under it, and each kind of noise has its own favourite place. Select a signal type to see how strongly each site picks it up, then click an electrode to see what its recording would look like.

  • Occipital (O1, O2): the visual cortex produces strong alpha when your eyes are closed, so these sites are the place to look for it.
  • Frontal (Fp1, Fp2): closest to the eyes, so blinks and eye movements show up here largest. Their electrical effect fades toward the back of the head.
  • Temporal (T7, T8): near the jaw and temple muscles, so tension and chewing add fast, irregular noise.
  • Central and parietal (Cz, Pz): where many cognitive ERPs, including the P3, are largest, along with sensorimotor activity at C3 and C4.

Two more things to keep in mind. Every EEG channel is a voltage difference relative to a reference electrode (often the mastoids or the average of all channels), so a noisy reference leaks into every channel. And because the skull blurs the electric field (volume conduction), neighbouring electrodes are highly correlated; a signal at Cz does not necessarily come from directly beneath Cz.

Putting it together

  • Choose electrodes near the generators of the activity you care about, and record eye and muscle channels to know what to discard.
  • Filter the continuous data with the mildest settings your question allows, then epoch it.
  • Reject or correct contaminated trials, then average the rest. Plan for enough trials: noise falls only as 1/√N.
